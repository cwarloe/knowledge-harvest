# Founder Capability Transfer: Research Notes

_Last updated: October 2026_

This note records the evidence base and market hypotheses behind the current Knowledge Harvest positioning. It is not intended to imply that every claim below has been fully market-validated.

## Research synthesis

### Founder dependency is a recognizable scaling problem

Service-business and systemization markets already sell against the problem of work, approvals, quality control, and decisions continuing to route through the founder as a company grows. This validates the pain but means Knowledge Harvest should **not** claim that "businesses should run without the founder" is a new category.

The more specific opportunity is the human-performance layer: responsibility has been delegated and the routine process may be documented, yet employees still lack some combination of judgment, standards, troubleshooting ability, situational awareness, or confidence needed for independent performance.

### Expert performance is not equivalent to observable procedure

Cognitive Task Analysis (CTA) and the Critical Decision Method provide an established basis for eliciting cues, goals, expectations, alternatives, judgments, and strategies used by experienced performers. This supports incident-based questions such as:

> You changed the employee's estimate. What did you notice that caused you to question it?

rather than broad prompts such as:

> Tell me everything you know about estimating.

### Learning is not the same as transfer

Training-transfer research distinguishes learning/acquisition from successful application in the workplace. That supports Knowledge Harvest's emphasis on testing whether the receiving employee can actually perform on routine and exception cases rather than counting completed training or documentation.

### Owner dependency can matter at exit as well as during growth

Business valuation and exit-advisory sources treat excessive dependence on a key owner as transferability/key-person risk. This suggests a secondary future buying moment: capability transfer in preparation for sale or succession. The initial positioning remains growth-focused.

## Candidate beachheads

### MSPs / technical service firms

Why attractive:

- technical founders often remain the senior escalation point;
- ticketing creates observable escalation data;
- outcomes such as escalations, reopen rates, resolution time, and technician independence can be measured;
- troubleshooting and exception handling contain significant tacit judgment;
- work artifacts already exist.

Current view: **best initial laboratory**, particularly because technical work can be understood and evaluated without first developing deep trade-domain expertise.

### Specialty contractors / HVAC

Why attractive:

- owner expertise often appears in estimating, troubleshooting, callbacks, quality review, and customer exceptions;
- mistakes and callbacks have visible economic consequences;
- scaling adds technicians but may not proportionally reduce owner involvement.

Current view: potentially a **larger/easier-to-sell eventual market**, but it requires more domain-specific learning and access.

### Professional services / agencies / accounting

Strong conceptual fit because founder expertise is often the product and junior work returns for review. These remain plausible later niches. Accounting and law add professional/regulatory boundaries; agencies and consulting have crowded operations/coaching markets.

## Marketing implications

Lead with symptoms and outcomes rather than knowledge-management terminology.

Promising language to test:

- **You hired people so you wouldn't have to do everything. Why does everything still come back to you?**
- **Your employees know the process. Why do they still need you?**
- **What can you see is wrong that your employee can't see yet?**
- **What did you know that they didn't?**

Avoid leading with:

- tacit knowledge management;
- cognitive task analysis;
- AI-powered knowledge capture;
- training transfer.

Those describe methods, not the owner's buying problem.

## Research caution

The current case is strong enough for an entrepreneurship-advisor conversation, but not yet proof of willingness to pay. The next evidence should prioritize buyer conversations, existing purchasing behavior, and a small test of the Escalation Audit rather than additional broad literature review.
